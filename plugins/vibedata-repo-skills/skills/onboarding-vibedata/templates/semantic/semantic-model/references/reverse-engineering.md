# Reverse-engineering and coverage

Use when onboarding a lakehouse into the canonical model, when a new mart lands, or when a table has no honest mapping. Everyday edits do not need this file.

Scope is the gold marts — facts, dims, aggregates, consumption marts. Staging views, intermediate models and seeds are upstream of the model and never appear as handles — but a seed a gold table joins is an input to that table, and a join against it that narrows the population is recorded with that table (see the LTS rule in `SKILL.md`). Build the inventory before partitioning documents: list every dbt model under the marts path, then read every mart's FROM/JOIN refs, because a table's population — and so whether it is an aggregate source or standalone — is only visible from its inputs. Every gold table either has a handle in `physical_tables` (with its `dbt_model`) or carries its reason on itself: unmappable tables get a `note` on the physical-table entry (truncated, wrong star, bridge treatment); partially mapped sources get a `note` on the logical source naming each refused column and why. Reasons ride the entries so they cannot drift from them. The rule binds both directions: reverse-engineering during onboarding enumerates and justifies gaps; forward-engineering a new mart registers it or justifies its absence in the same change.

When SQL and established decisions determine exactly one honest disposition, record it with an evidence-backed note. If grain, population or business meaning has multiple plausible interpretations, ask for a ruling before writing that mapping: location, evidence, options and recommendation. Keep the unresolved item in the onboarding review and coverage report while independent models proceed. An unanswered question is not permission to assume a mapping or to declare coverage complete. Preserve existing entries pending the decision; never change mart code to make a model pass.


For each aggregate or consumption mart you register as a logical source, write its content from the SQL in the same pass, not afterwards. `content_levels` comes from its `GROUP BY`: one entry per dimension role it groups by, naming the level that column keys (a `date_trunc('month', ...)` is the month level; a zone natural key is the zone level), with `via` set to the relationship the mart's join actually follows and `key_columns` set to the grouping columns that carry the level's key (`key` names a non-primary key when the mart groups by that one, such as a payment method's name). A grouping column that keys no declared level is recorded in the `note`, not invented as a level. `content_filter` comes from its `WHERE` **plus every inner join**: record null-key loss, unmatched non-null keys and additional join predicates. A `<key> IS NOT NULL` filter alone is sufficient only when established referential integrity and the join predicate prove that it describes every dropped row; otherwise state the full restriction and retain any unverified data claim. Window bounds use the model `variables` bound to the dbt vars, never literal dates. Then extend the mapped test that holds the source filters with the source's bound totals, and point the `note` at it. A `HAVING` or other group-level cut sets `navigable: false` and needs the ADR that rules the population.

Declare levels from the dbt metadata and the business's intent, never from what the data happens to satisfy: follow the four mapping steps in `SKILL.md` S5a (candidate keys from `primary_key`, `unique` tests and constraints; assign each to its level and choose the primary; non-identifying readable columns become attributes; hierarchies from the drill paths). Then run `ossie.py level-tests` with the mapped model directory and test-file path, and run the mapped dbt level-test selector, and record what it reports as data findings, leaving the declaration as written. Give every metric an `aggregation`; write a rate as a `derived_expression` over the metrics for its numerator and denominator, and refuse the rate columns aggregates carry. A mart column that averages, maximises or picks the last of per-group values (an average daily count, a peak day, a closing balance, a mean of driver rates) is a dimension-based aggregation: declare it as `based_on_dimensions` across the inner level with that rule, and refuse the column, because it cannot roll up.

A mart that filters its rows (billable only) can supply a metric whose `filter` states that filter, or an unfiltered metric only when the requested query imposes the missing restriction. Record the source's full `content_filter`; a binding does not make it eligible for unrestricted queries. Never rename an unfiltered metric to disguise a narrower population. A bridge mart (one row per member and child, with a share column) joins its star as a weighted bridge, with a test that the shares sum to 1. A dimension mart at a coarser grain than its dimension (a month calendar, a segment table) is a coarser source of that dimension, not a new dataset. A mart column that rolls a window over rows (`rows between 6 preceding`) is refused in favour of a `ROLLING` metric.

Ask versus note-only: when the mart SQL leaves exactly one honest disposition (wrong star by its own header, truncation that defeats every mapping), record the `note` directly under the coverage rule. When two or more dispositions are plausible, or the analysis itself is uncertain (contradictory grain evidence, undefined treatment), ask under this rule.
